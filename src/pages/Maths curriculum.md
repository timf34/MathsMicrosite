# Ireland is about to destroy senior maths education

Last month the NCCA, Ireland’s national body for teaching and examination standards, released the [new draft specification for Leaving Certificate mathematics](https://ncca.ie/en/senior-cycle/curriculum-developments/mathematics/).

If this draft (or something like it) is implemented, it could destroy quality mathematical education in Ireland. These are the words of Prof. Pauline Mellon, a member of the NCCA’s mathematics committee who resigned this year:

> *“**This draft curriculum should not be progressed.** If it, or a variant of it, is adopted, it will significantly harm mathematics education and disadvantage the tens of thousands of students who will take it each year. It will further reduce us in international comparisons and will lead to a reduction of standards for third- level courses requiring quantitative skills, with knock-on consequences downstream for many key industries.”*

We think she is right, and indeed the new documents are perhaps even worse than she says. If implemented, the abilities of our students will be called into doubt by the world's best universities and employers. Ireland’s reputation as a place where businesses can access genuinely skilled workers will be threatened. And our ability to engage with the frontier of economic and technological development will be severely hampered.

This is happening as part of [a wider reform to the Leaving Cert](https://www.irishtimes.com/opinion/2026/09/14/breda-obrien-rollout-of-the-reformed-leaving-cert-should-be-suspended/). There are many other worrying trends in these reforms, which we were planning to write a general article about. But the draft maths specification is *so* bad, and could have *such* disastrous consequences, that we’ve had to focus our attention. These issues cannot be fixed via the usual process of consultation: the government must urgently step in to stop this draft spec from being progressed, and send the NCCA back to the drawing board, with clear instructions to follow the advice of mathematicians.

### 40% of maths Leaving Cert will be subjective and gameable

The biggest and most dangerous change of all has to do with how Leaving Cert maths will be assessed. Under the proposals, at the end of fifth year, students will take part in a one-day ‘Investigating with Mathematics’ event where they will ‘consider a real-life issue with Mathematics’.

This will be graded against verbal and qualitative descriptors, instead of a rigourous mark scheme. That is to say, students will be graded on such things as their ability to ‘provide clear accurate interpretation’ or ‘identify all key features of \[a\] visual pattern’. And instead of being given numerical marks, students will instead be assigned one of three possible bands per criterion: ‘High’, ‘Moderate’, or ‘Low’.

This will be 40% of their grade.[^1]

If this is implemented, this could destroy the reputation of Leaving Cert mathematics. The problems are many:

* ***It is subjective and thus gameable.*** Teachers who are ‘in the know’ about how the examiners approach this task will be able to coach their students to meet the subjective criteria, getting them to ‘jump through hoops’ without actually showing any original mathematical ability.[^2]
* ***Marking will necessarily be crude and simplified.*** When the only options are ‘High’, ‘Moderate’, and ‘Low’, there’s no way to make a distinction between good students and truly *excellent* students: both are ‘High’. And on the other end, students who seriously struggle with maths but are trying their best, who might pick up working-out marks in an exam, will be bucketed as ‘Low’ along with those who don’t care and put no effort in.  
* ***It will be unreliable.*** Subjective rubrics produce a wider range of results than strict markschemes: different examiners rarely disagree over whether a student’s calculation was correct, but they will inevitably disagree over how well students ‘identify features of a pattern’. With 60,000 students sitting Leaving Cert maths every year, even a small divergence between examiners could result in thousands of students being mismarked.  
* ***It will be just as stressful for students as an exam.*** Usually the main benefit of non-exam assessments is that students feel less pressure, because it’s spread out over a longer period. But a one-day timed event will be just as stressful. Indeed, because the marking will be crude and subjective and unreliable, it’ll probably be *more* stressful: students will have almost no way of knowing if they’re prepared or not, or whether they’ll get an examiner who’s stricter than average.  
* ***Much of the assessment is just not well-defined.*** One-quarter of ‘Investigating with Mathematics’ will be focussed on the topic of ‘patterns’: this is simply not a mathematically-defined concept, and the NCCA has made no effort to define it themselves.

But, most importantly, ***it assesses the wrong things.*** A student whose mathematical reasoning contains mistakes, but who submits a visually-polished report with lots of nice charts, could get a ‘Moderate’ in ‘Mathematical reasoning’ but a ‘High’ in ‘Communicating the findings’. A student who spends a lot of time ensuring their maths is impeccable, and so has less time to add charts and nice visuals, could get a ‘High’ in ‘Mathematical reasoning’ but only a ‘Moderate’ in ‘Communicating the findings’. These two students could get *the exact same grade*, even though one of them showed more skill in mathematics: the other one made up for it using their skill in producing pretty Word documents.[^3]

A high mark (H2 or H1) in mathematics would no longer be a guarantee that a student is reliably good at mathematics. A middling student could get lucky with a kind examiner for ‘Investigating with Mathematics’, be coached effectively by their teachers, and offset their mathematical weakness with nice charts or effective communication or an eye for ‘patterns’.

Meanwhile, if a really hard-working and mathematically gifted teenager had teachers who object to ‘coaching’, a harsh examiner, and/or a weakness in non-mathematical skills, they’d be hamstrung. If such a student got 74% or less on ‘Investigating with Mathematics’ because of these factors, it would be literally impossible for them to get a H1, no matter what they did in sixth year—*even if* they got a perfect 100% in every single exam.

Some of these problems are present in other subjects, of course. Marking in subjects like music, or English, is often criticised for being subjective and vague. But two points should be taken into account:

1. In many humanities subjects, there is just no alternative to somewhat-subjective rubric-based assessment. But in maths, we actually have a rigourous and objective way to mark\! We should be using it.  
2. At least in a subject like English, the subjective assessment is trying to assess students *on their English skills*. The worst issue with ‘Investigating with Mathematics’ is that students will be graded on communication, visual aesthetics, and many other things that *aren’t* mathematical ability.

Employers and universities look for people who *know mathematics*. That is why they trust Leaving Cert maths: it is an imperfect but mostly-reliable indicator of someone’s mathematical abilities. That trust could be destroyed overnight by these reforms. Employers and overseas universities would place much less trust in Irish qualifications, and thus accept or hire fewer people from Ireland; meanwhile, Irish universities would be unable to select the most capable students, and would struggle to keep up in international academia.

Meanwhile, students would have much less reason to work hard and properly learn maths. There would just be fewer benefits to putting in effort, thanks to subjective assessments and crude, simplistic marking rubrics. Meanwhile, many students lacking essential mathematical knowledge could still pick up marks in ‘Investigating with Mathematics’, and might even get a decent pass. Thanks to these incentives, the actual mathematical knowledge of Irish school leavers would drop, year-on-year. Not only would employers and universities stop trusting Irish graduates’ mathematical ability; increasingly, they’d be *right* not to trust it.

### Important content has been gutted, and nothing added in its place

‘Investigating with Mathematics’ would be disastrous on its own, enough to destroy the rigour and reputation of Leaving Cert maths. But—perhaps to make room for the inevitable coaching for ‘Investigating with Mathematics’—the examined portion of the course has also been cut to the bone.

Some of the most important content on the old maths spec has been removed completely. In particular, all material on complex numbers has been cut, as well as all mention of proofs by induction, and all geometric proofs. This is not just a random assortment of topics: these are the first places in the course where students get the experience of ‘higher’ mathematical reasoning.

What do we mean by that? Well, before Leaving Cert, maths education is focussed on relatively concrete topics that most people have some understanding of: number and shape. But the kind of mathematics that is actually taught in universities—and which you need for many tech and science and engineering jobs—relies on the power of *abstraction*: defining and investigating new concepts and structures, like the [complex plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number), that might not make intuitive everyday sense but which serve as extremely powerful tools for solving problems.

Likewise, most school maths is about *calculation*, which is certainly a big part of mathematics. But the more central idea in mathematics is *proof*: rather than just calculating an answer in a specific case, you demonstrate (beyond doubt) that *all* problems like this have such-and-such answers; rather than just uncritically applying a formula, you show *why* the formula is true, and get a deeper understanding of how it works.

The draft documents pay lip-service to these ideas. But by cutting abstract topics like complex numbers, and proofs in both geometry and number theory, the proposed reforms would leave students with no *training* whatsoever in abstraction or proof.[^4]

Perhaps other material has been added to make up for all this? No: there are no major new topics on the spec.

Well, perhaps old topics are being investigated in more depth? Again, no. If you were forced to be as kind as possible, you could *maybe* say that the treatment of integration at Higher Level is *slightly* more detailed than it was before. But even then, these changes take up only one page of the 70-page spec.[^5] Every other topic is either unchanged, or (more often) has actively been dumbed down—statistics, sequences and series, trigonometry, etc.

And it’s worse than that. Even where the *content* has not been dumbed down, the *way* students are taught the content will change dramatically: in about a dozen topics, it is no longer expected that students will solve problems themselves. Students learning about these topics, from functions to optimisation to synthetic geometry, will instead be explicitly given the answer, and just have to double-check it. Genuine, open-ended problem-solving is no longer a requirement.

### Ireland needs quality mathematics education

Sometimes people try to distinguish (a) practical ‘numeracy skills’ that students will use in the wider world, from (b) the kind of formal, often-abstract maths that is done in universities. And they argue that schools should focus on (a) to prepare our teenagers with the skills they will actually need. The draft specs show a lot of evidence of this kind of thinking.

One way to answer it is to say that, even if it’s not practically relevant, it is important to be exposed to higher mathematical concepts for a well-rounded civic education. After all, most people don’t need to have read Shakespeare for their jobs, but we as a society want citizens who have some familiarity with his works, which are among the greatest achievements of world literature. Likewise, practicalities aside, we as a society should want citizens who have some familiarity with ‘higher’ mathematics, which includes some of humanity’s greatest and most beautiful intellectual achievements.

We agree with this. But there’s also a more hard-headed response: many of our teenagers ***will*** practically use (b) in the wider world, if they are actually given the opportunity to learn it—an opportunity they won’t have if the proposed spec is progressed.

Ireland is increasingly aiming to have an economy with heavy reliance on technical sectors like engineering, science, pharma, software, etc. We believe that this is absolutely the right way forward for the country. But it is a fact that jobs in these sectors often demand ‘higher’, abstract, formal mathematical knowledge. Complex numbers are vitally important problem-solving tools for all sorts of engineering jobs. Scientific research jobs in pharma and other sectors demand a strong familiarity with statistics. Modern AI is built on linear algebra—vectors and matrices—a topic that was removed from the maths curriculum a decade ago. Ireland needs these skills: we should be adding topics like vectors and matrices back in, not stripping even more topics out.

Students who want to work in these sectors will have to learn ‘higher’ mathematical topics at some point. If they don’t appear on the Leaving Cert, it’ll be down to university and college lecturers (already spread thin) to dilute their courses even more to make room for a few hours dedicated to these topics—topics that really need several weeks of dedicated attention. Inevitably, Irish workers will enter the job market with less understanding and a weaker grasp of the ideas, and they will be less able to apply them in their work. And this will all be made much worse by the unrigourous, gameable, subjective ‘Investigating with Mathematics’.

Everyday numeracy (stuff like basic finance and probability) should perhaps be the focus at Foundation Level. But at Ordinary Level—and certainly at Higher Level—mathematics education should be focussed on *teaching mathematics*.

The point goes deeper than just jobs. The modern world is increasingly reliant on extremely complex and specialised technologies. For a country in the modern world, having a cohort of citizens who are able to understand these technologies isn’t just a nice-to-have: it is essential if we want to actually shape the trajectory of these technologies, to ensure Ireland has the ability to shape its own future. Saying maths knowledge is good for jobs is correct, but it’s a bit like saying we teach kids how to read and write so they can get jobs. It’s partly true, but more fundamentally we teach kids to read and write because, without literate citizens, a country cannot interact with other advanced nations on a level playing field. The same is true of mathematics.

### The effects of educational reforms can be huge

A lot of people—even people who are otherwise sympathetic to our points—might nonetheless doubt that there’s much to worry about. Yes, maybe the draft spec is dumbed-down, and maybe the new assessment is too subjective and vague. But ultimately, maybe after a few years of adjustment, the cleverest and hardest-working kids will master the system and find a way to get to the top. The new spec won’t be *great*, but it won't be a crisis.

We wish this complacency were justified. But increasingly, we have strong evidence from around the world that educational reforms don’t just cause temporary disruption: bad reforms can destroy a country’s knowledge base.

For a nearby example, we can look to Scotland, which in 2010 made a radical overhaul to its school system, the so-called ‘Curriculum for Excellence’. In the intervening sixteen years, [Scottish students have fallen behind](https://www.enlighten.scot/scotland-and-the-2025-pisa-results/) by the equivalent of eight months of reading, 14 months of science and 19 months of mathematics. Let’s repeat that: being taught the Curriculum for Excellence instead of the old Scottish curriculum is the equivalent of *missing a year and a half of maths*.

And even just looking at the highest achievers—the top 10% of students each year, in terms of assessment marks—they too have fallen behind: the top 10% of the most recent Scottish cohort now have fallen behind by a *full year* in maths, compared to the highest achievers in 2018\. Some of this is a post-covid decline that has happened in many countries; but if you [use England as a comparison](https://www.heraldscotland.com/news/26532939.pisa-2025-pays-make-comparisons-england/) (same island, same economy, same negative exposure to social media, the only difference is the curriculum), it is clear that the Curriculum for Excellence has caused an immense decline in Scottish abilities.

The knock-on effects in higher education have been huge. [Only a quarter of students at Scotland’s leading universities are actually Scottish](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2024/dec/26/scottish-students-at-edinburgh-university-start-support-group-to-counter-alienation); and attempts to get more Scots into university have foundered on the fact that many Scottish kids, especially the poorest, [just have not received a good enough education for university](https://www.thetimes.com/uk/scotland/article/snp-drive-to-get-more-poor-students-into-university-unachievable-nd52t0xcj). And we can expect that the effects on the job market, and Scottish society more generally, will start to accumulate in coming years as students educated under the new system come to make up a larger proportion of the population. This is a country that previously was among the best-educated in the world.

It might be true that the smartest Scottish kids still know how to get good results. A university or employer might still trust a high-scoring Scottish school leaver to be generally smart and capable. But a school leaver with good results from another country (e.g., England) can be trusted to be generally smart *and* also to be nearly two years ahead in terms of their mathematical and scientific training. The choice is a no-brainer. Over time, Scottish firms will lower their expectations, and international firms will be less likely to choose Scotland.

The same point can be illustrated with dozens of examples, from [Quebec](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S027277571400034X) to [Japan](https://www.ft.com/content/0b21280e-b37c-11df-81aa-00144feabdc0), and from economic research showing that there is a [large and probably-causal association](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2010/01/the-high-cost-of-low-educational-performance_g1ghc4d1/9789264077485-en.pdf) between better educational outcomes and economic growth.[^7] Ill-thought-out changes to a country’s school system, and *especially* to maths education, can be hugely damaging.

### We should be worried about the process

And unfortunately, this change to the Irish maths specification seems extremely poorly-thought-out. There are reasons to seriously worry, not just about the content of the draft spec, but about the process that produced it.

Two of the experts on the NCCA’s maths committee—[Professor Pauline Mellon](https://people.ucd.ie/pauline.mellon) of UCD, and [Dr Aoibhinn Ní Shúilleabháin](https://www.dcu.ie/stemeducationinnovationglobalstudies/people/aoibhinn-ni-shuilleabhain) of DCU—have resigned, unwilling to be associated with the draft curriculum. And since their resignations, we’ve seen concerning revelations about the process by which it was constructed.

In the first place, the decision to make 40% of the subject a non-exam assessment was not the result of consultation with educators and subject-matter experts in maths. It was a [ministerial decision made in 2022](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-education/press-releases/minister-foley-announces-plan-for-reform-of-senior-cycle-education-equity-and-excellence-for-all/) and applied to *all* subjects, without detailed examination of the appropriateness for this rule in different contexts.

Of course, it’s incumbent on civil servants to work hard to try to implement ministerial decisions as far as they can. But they should be willing to listen to experts on how, exactly, to implement the decision. And if it turns out that the decision is simply unworkable, civil servants should be willing to go back to the minister and argue for a change of policy.

But the testimony especially of Dr Ní Shúilleabháin suggests that there was little scope for expert input into the shape of ‘Investigating with Mathematics’, never mind rejecting it altogether: [‘conversations were limited to … shoe-horning assessments into a format that does not make sense’](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7500104021907664896/).

This point about conversations being limited points to a more general problem with the process, which the NCCA staff seem to have treated essentially as a formality, rather than something that might shape the specification. Dr Ní Shúilleabháin has written of the maths subject group that [‘group decisions were not always recorded, handpicked opinions appeared in drafts and input from some members was consistently ignored.’](https://www.irishtimes.com/opinion/2026/09/05/aoibhinn-ni-shuilleabhain-why-i-walked-away-from-the-leaving-cert-maths-reform-group/) (The claim that group decisions were not actually recorded is consistent with the [extremely short, bland, and uninformative minutes](https://ncca.ie/en/senior-cycle/curriculum-developments/mathematics/) that are available for the maths committee’s meetings.)

One particularly worrying piece of testimony came from Prof. Mellon, who [told RTÉ](https://www.rte.ie/radio/radio1/clips/4fa906df-0780-481a-89db-b4b8009cab92/) that members of the maths committee were not actually provided with the responses to an earlier consultation. Instead, they were provided with a ‘summary’ written by the NCCA that purported to represent the jist of consultation responses; but when committee members asked to see the full responses, to understand things more fully and check if the ‘summary’ was accurate, the NCCA refused.

(As an indication: the same policy was applied to the science subject groups, but in that case the original consultation responses were later revealed under Freedom of Information. We agree with the Irish Science Teachers’ Association that the NCCA ‘summary’ document bears [‘little resemblance’](https://ista.ie/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/ISTA-Submission-to-Curricum-Reform-in-the-Senior-Cycle-Discussion-V2.pdf) to the actual consultation responses.)

And finally, and most blatantly, the draft specification is *full of blatant mathematical errors*. Obviously, mistakes happen when a complicated document is being drafted and redrafted by many hands; this is inevitable. But the very worrisome thing is that both Prof. Mellon and Dr Ní Shúilleabháin testified that they (and other committee members) *noticed the errors*, tried to get the NCCA to fix them, and were ignored\! These are not the normal understandable mistakes that are inherent to a document like this; they give us good reason to be worried about the process.

Is it possible that these ‘errors’ were just attempts to simplify complicated concepts for students? We hope not. The two of us have gone through the draft specification and have found many of these errors (we list a few of the most prominent in an [appendix](#appendix-mathematical-errors-in-the-draft-specification) below); lots of them are extremely basic and crude. If the NCCA think this is an appropriate level of simplification for 18-year-olds, this would be extremely concerning: it suggests that, as Prof. Mellon has said, they want to ‘make mathematics friendlier by making it less mathematical.’[^8]

### Ministers need to intervene

Thanks to the resignations, the Irish Mathematical Society [openly condemning the draft spec](https://www.irishmathsoc.org/business/?file=lccr), and the discovery of the blatant errors, the proposed maths reforms have been mired in controversy. In response, [the NCCA have tried to emphasise](https://www.rte.ie/radio/radio1/clips/6d6ae6da-6614-4805-a1c3-b4b800a65ffe/) that this is a draft specification, out for consultation, and that there is a process of engagement by which it can be improved.

The issue with this argument is twofold. First: we have seen how the NCCA handles consultations, and more broadly the way their processes work; it does not inspire confidence. We know that NCCA staff have, inadvertently or willingly, misrepresented consultation responses; failed to take on board the views expressed in consultations; and refused to reveal the full details of consultation responses to experts, so that they could give proper feedback. More generally, experts testify to feeling railroaded towards the NCCA’s pre-made decisions: that content would be cut, and that ‘Investigating with Mathematics’ would be introduced. Two mathematical experts have resigned precisely because they do not trust the NCCA’s processes to improve this spec.

Second, and more importantly: even if we trusted the NCCA’s processes more than we do, there is no amount of tweaking this document that could produce a good specification. It is too fundamentally flawed: hollowed-out, dumbed-down, and badly assessed. It does not reflect any sense of what mathematics is; or why it is a significant and important subject; or what it is most important that students learn to understand mathematics.

From the cuts made, it is clear NCCA simply does not believe abstraction or proof are worth training students in; even if they quickly added some stuff about complex numbers and induction back in, there would be no sense that mathematics builds towards abstraction. And the form of assessment is simply unworkable for mathematics. The most blatant errors might be fixed during the consultation—they probably will be—but the real problem is the structure of these documents.

The Government must act: make the NCCA to throw out these drafts, and send them back to the drawing board. And just as importantly, the Government must intervene in the *process* of making a new specification, so that we don’t just have the same rigamarole over again.

Rules have to be laid down to give mathematicians—the experts in this subject—a decisive say in the new curriculum. These decisionmaking processes must have rules requiring that decisions made by the subject-matter experts are recorded, publicly acknowledged, and taken into serious consideration. The advisory mathematicians must be able to share documents with each other and take minutes without NCCA interference. And they must have wide freedom to consider the shape of the new specification, including freedom to make a new decision regarding the nature and extent of non-exam assessment.

This will be painful, both for the NCCA and for the Government (who will have to go back on their previous public commitment to the 40% non-exam component for all subjects). But the risks of letting this process continue to play out are just too great. If Ireland is to continue to be a rich, advanced, technical country punching above its weight, not just a sluggish ex–tax haven, it *needs* technical expertise and skills; and technical expertise and skills *need* rigourous mathematics education.

### Appendix: Mathematical errors in the draft specification

These are a number of the errors we found in the draft documents published by the NCCA. They are not the only mistakes we found, and we are certain there are other mistakes we didn’t spot; the list is just to give you a flavour of this document.

* Page 17 discusses “rational expressions of the form (ax \+ b)/(cx \+ d) ± (px \+ q)/(rx \+ s), where a, b, c, d, e, f, p, q, r ∈ ℤ”. There is no e or f in the expression, but s—which *is* in the expression—is undefined and unconstrained.  
* Page 22 says that students should learn that P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A); but this is not true if A and B are equally likely.  
* Page 31 says that students should understand the ‘application of axioms 1–6’ of Euclidean geometry. Famously, there are [five axioms of Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry#Axioms); indeed, the document [*Geometry for post–primary school mathematics*](https://www.curriculumonline.ie/getmedia/ed7de7ab-2376-44f0-9c7c-b511e470c0a7/Appendix_B_English.pdf), explicitly referred to in the draft spec, has five axioms, and axiom 6 does not exist.  
* Page 37 says the central limit theorem is what “justifies generalising from a sample to a population”. This is untrue: *taking* *random samples* is what justifies generalising to a population. That is much more basic and intuitive than the central limit theorem, which is about the distribution of the sample means of a set of [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed_random_variables) variables.  
* Page 49 claims that rational functions k/(ax \+ b) are “defined for all x ∈ ℝ”. In fact they’re undefined when x \= \-b/a.  
* Page 55, which is the Ordinary Level spec, defines the inverse of a function in terms of the equations (f ∘ f−1)(x) \= x and (f−1 ∘ f)(x) \= x. These equations are correct, but they rely on the symbol ∘, representing function composition—and this *is not on the Ordinary Level spec*, only Higher Level. Ordinary Level students would be taught these equations without ever being taught how the symbol ∘ works.

[^1]:   It’s not even fully clear from the spec if ‘Investigating with Mathematics’ will be a group task or an individual task. Most likely it will not be (especially based on Junior Cycle precedent), and certainly we *hope* that 40% of Leaving Cert maths grades won’t come down to a group project; but the spec is so unclear that the public really has to rely on *hope*. There is a reference to a supplemental document, *Guidance to Support the Completion of the Investigating with Mathematics Tasks*, but this doesn’t exist yet.

[^2]:  This is exactly what happened when England experimented with non-exam assessment in GCSE maths (the equivalent of Junior Cert, ages 14–16): students were being taught how to [‘jump through hoops’](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/education-20166671) to meet the criteria, rather than actually being taught maths. England eventually [scrapped the non-exam assessment](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/education-22841266) after hard experience; Ireland should take advantage of our neighbour’s experience and scrap it now, without having to go through the trouble ourselves.

[^3]:  This is assuming that the various rubrics (like ‘Communicating the findings’ and ‘Mathematical reasoning’ etc.) are given equal weight. Again, maybe the guidance will say otherwise; but the public currently has no way of knowing. And ultimately, even if the most subjective rubrics (e.g., the ones to do with formulating questions and communication) are given less weight, they will be given *some* weight; and even the least subjective rubrics are still very subjective and crude.

[^4]:  Students would also have no rigourous exposure to statistics at all: statistics has been cut entirely from the exams, and only appears in the course through ‘Investigating with Mathematics’. Again, this is essential for further study in the sciences and for many jobs.

[^5]:  Further, some topics have been sufficiently dumbed down at Higher Level that they are now suitable for Ordinary Level, and so in places the syllabus is the same between the two where previously it differed (e.g., ‘Deductive reasoning in geometry’). Technically this is additional content at Ordinary Level, but (a) it’s not genuinely new content, it’s old content that’s been dumbed down enough to cross the boundary between Higher and Ordinary; and (b) in every case, this ‘new’ content is less deep and important than the things that have cut from Ordinary Level to make way for it.

[^6]:  In the few places where students *are* still expected to come up with solutions on their own, the spec makes it explicit, and uses the verbs ‘to find’ or ‘to solve’ as well as ‘to justify’. So ‘proposed solutions’ definitely means ‘solutions proposed by the examiners’, not ‘proposed by the student themselves’. See also U2 (pp. 13–14) and the definition of ‘justify’ in the Glossary (p. 70).

[^7]:  The evidence that this relationship is causal is not *extremely* robust, and might potentially be contested. But there is a [separate literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0014292114001433) showing a correlation between cognitive skills, especially mathematical skills, and *individual* wages, which also seems likely to be causal (although equally you might potentially contest this). And the theoretical argument for why we should expect a causal effect, given above (firms would prefer someone who is smart *and knowledgeable* over someone who is only smart) is intuitive and supported by the evidence. Altogether I think we can be pretty confident that there is a large causal effect, even if we should be uncertain as to the *exact* amount of the association that is causal.

[^8]:  The Education Minister, Hildegarde Naughton, responded to an RTÉ question about these errors by saying [‘I’m assured that there are no errors in it.’](https://www.rte.ie/radio/radio1/clips/9ccbb621-5dbf-4cf1-9413-b4b80126e3b4/) We expect that, because a minister’s life is busy, she just had not had a chance to review the specific errors yet, and was relying on the NCCA’s word for it. But we hope she does take the time to [review the errors](#appendix-mathematical-errors-in-the-draft-specification), because it is beyond question that these *are* errors, very crude and basic mathematical inaccuracies; there is no room for dispute on that point.
